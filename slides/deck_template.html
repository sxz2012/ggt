Opening · Symmetry

Where we are going

A symmetry of an object is a move that leaves it looking exactly as it did. To understand an object, find its symmetries: for a cube, a polygon, a line, or something abstract like a set of functions.

  1. A puzzle. How many ways can you turn a cube, and how do the turns combine?
  2. What a group is, and why every collection of symmetries is one.
  3. A tour of examples: polygons (Dn), permutations (Sn), clocks (ℤ/nℤ), the integers, the grid, matrices, and free groups. Each comes with the picture it is the symmetries of.
  4. Maps between groups: homomorphisms, kernels, normal subgroups, quotients, and the first isomorphism theorem.
  5. Writing a group down: generators, relations, and the Cayley graph as the picture of a presentation.
The one sentence
“Group” and “the symmetries of something” are the same idea seen from two sides. Symmetries can be combined (do one, then another) and undone; a group is exactly a set with those two features. Every example today is both a group and a set of symmetries.

Opening · The cube

A puzzle: how many ways can you turn a cube?

A symmetry of the cube is what you get by picking it up, turning it, and putting it back in exactly the same place. Mirror images are not allowed: you cannot do those with your hands.

  • Skewer the cube through the centres of two opposite faces and turn by a multiple of 90°.
  • Or skewer it through the midpoints of two opposite edges, or through two opposite corners.
  • Counting: corner A can be sent to any of the 8 corners, and once you have chosen, the cube can still spin about that corner in 3 ways. So there are 8 × 3 = 24 symmetries.
Watch out
Counting is not understanding. What is a good way to list the 24? And if we do one turn and then another, we get a third turn: where is it on the list? The next three slides answer this.

Opening · Why multiples of 90°, 120°, 180°

Look straight down the skewer

Why can you only turn by 90° about a face axis, and not by, say, 45°? Look straight down the axis you are turning about. The cube's outline becomes a flat shape, and a turn is a symmetry of the cube exactly when it is a symmetry of that outline.

  • Down a face axis the outline is a square. It returns to itself after 90°; after 45° its corners stick out. So only multiples of 90° work.
  • Down a long diagonal the outline is a regular hexagon centred on the near corner, whose three edges make 120° angles. Multiples of 120°.
  • Down an edge axis the outline is a rectangle with sides 1 and √2, not a square. Only the half turn.
The count, again
1 identity + 3 face axes × 3 turns + 4 long diagonals × 2 turns + 6 edge axes × 1 turn = 24. The same number as before, now organised by axis.

Opening · The four long diagonals

The idea that solves the puzzle: turns of the cube are permutations of 4 diagonals

Draw the four long diagonals of the cube. Two facts:

  1. Every turn of the cube moves long diagonals to long diagonals, so it permutes d1, d2, d3, d4.
  2. Every permutation of the four diagonals comes from exactly one turn.
Why fact 2 is true
Swapping two diagonals is the half turn about the axis through the midpoints of the two edges that join them, so every swap is a turn. Any permutation is a product of swaps, so every permutation is a turn. Both sides have 24 elements, so no two turns give the same permutation.
The payoff
A permutation is a bijection, so “do one turn, then another” is just composition of functions: mechanical. The 24 moves have become the symmetric group S₄. In the lab below, the readout “where the cube is” is exactly this product.

1.1 Groups · The definition

Definition of a group

Definition
A multiplication on a set G is a function G × G → G; the image of (g, h) is written gh. A group is a set G with a multiplication such that
  1. Identity. There is an element 1 ∈ G with 1g = g1 = g for all g.
  2. Inverses. For each g there is an element g⁻¹ with gg⁻¹ = g⁻¹g = 1.
  3. Associativity. (fg)h = f(gh) for all f, g, h.
Why this is about symmetries
For the symmetries of the cube, define gh as “do h, then do g”, like the composition g∘h. The identity is “leave it alone”; the inverse of g is “undo g”; associativity holds because composing functions is associative. So the symmetries of anything form a group. The symbol 1 is a name, not the number one: in ℤ the identity is 0.
Watch out
Nothing in the definition says gh = hg. Groups where that always holds are called abelian. The cube's group is not abelian: a quarter turn about a vertical axis followed by a half turn about an edge axis differs from the same two moves in the other order.
Generators
A subset S generates G if every element of G is a product of elements of S and their inverses. The two reflections on the next slide generate the whole dihedral group; the neighbour swaps generate all of Sn.
{{fig:figcube}}
The symmetries of the cube satisfy all three axioms: do nothing, undo, and compose.
Animationoptional · the axioms acted out on the cube

1.1 Groups · The dihedral group

Dn: symmetries of a regular n-gon

Dn is the set of symmetries of a regular n-gon in the plane, now including reflections (you can flip a flat polygon over). It has n rotations and n reflections, so |Dn| = 2n.

  • s: reflection in a line through the centre and a vertex. t: reflection in the line rotated from it by π/n.
  • s and t generate Dn: every symmetry is a product of s's and t's. A rotation by m clicks is (st)m; each reflection is a rotation followed by s.
  • s² = 1: reflecting twice puts everything back, so s is its own inverse.
Why st is one click
Reflecting in two lines that meet at angle θ is the same as rotating by 2θ about their intersection. Here θ = π/n, so st is the rotation by 2π/n.
Notation
Some books call this group D2n, naming it by its size. This book names it by the polygon.

1.1 Groups · The symmetric group

Sn: all permutations of {1, …, n}

Sn is the set of permutations (bijections) of {1, …, n}, with multiplication given by composition. It is the symmetry group of n loose points: no distances or shape to respect, so every rearrangement counts, and |Sn| = n!.

Cycle notation. (i₁ i₂ … ik) sends each listed number to the next, the last back to the first, and leaves everything else fixed: (1 2 4) sends 1→2, 2→4, 4→1. Every permutation is a product of disjoint cycles, such as (1 2 4)(3 5) in S₆.

Multiplying is composing, right factor first. For (2 6)·(1 2 4)(3 5):

x123456
after (1 2 4)(3 5)245136
after (2 6)645132

Reading the last row: 1→6→2→4→1 and 3↔5, so the product is (1 6 2 4)(3 5).

Watch out
Apply the left factor first and you get (1 2 4 6)(3 5), a different element. Order matters, and the convention is “right factor first”, matching gh = “h, then g”.

1.1 Groups · Transpositions · Exercises 1 and 2

Neighbour swaps generate Sn

A transposition is a 2-cycle such as (3 5): it swaps two things and fixes the rest. Important fact: Sn is generated by the adjacent transpositions (i i+1), 1 ≤ i ≤ n−1. (We used this on the cube: every permutation of the diagonals is a product of swaps.)

In a strand diagram each crossing swaps two neighbouring positions, so it is an adjacent transposition. Read the crossings top to bottom and write them right to left: for (1 2 4)(3 5) the diagram gives (3 4)(1 2)(4 5)(2 3)(3 4).

Exercise 1, as an algorithm
Any strand diagram can be drawn with its crossings at different heights, and then it is a product of adjacent transpositions. Equivalently: bubble sort. Take the arrangement the permutation produces and sort it back into order using only swaps of neighbours. Each swap is an adjacent transposition, and the list of swaps is a factorisation.
Exercise 2
Which other Sn are the symmetries of a shape? The regular tetrahedron's rotations and reflections give S₄; in general the regular simplex with n corners gives Sn. Higher cubes do not: the 4-dimensional cube has 192 rotations, and 5! = 120.

1.1 Groups · The integers modulo n · Exercise 3

ℤ/nℤ: add, then wrap around

Definition
For an integer n > 1, ℤ/nℤ = {0, 1, …, n−1} with the multiplication “add a and b, and subtract n if the result is n or more”. The identity is 0; the inverse of m ≠ 0 is n − m.

Three familiar cases: n = 12 is clock arithmetic (9 o'clock plus 4 hours is 1 o'clock); n = 10 is “only keep the last digit”; n = 2 is light-switch arithmetic, where 1 means flip and flip plus flip is nothing.

Associativity
The definition is by cases, but (a + b) + c and a + (b + c) are both the remainder of the ordinary integer a + b + c on division by n, and taking remainders is consistent with integer addition.
Exercise 3
If gcd(m, n) = 1 then {m} generates ℤ/nℤ. Why: there are integers u, v with um + vn = 1, so u·m ≡ 1, and from 1 you reach everything. Conversely if d = gcd(m, n) > 1, every multiple of m is a multiple of d on the clock, so 1 is never reached.
Subgroup
A subgroup is a subset containing 1 that is closed under products and inverses. Starting from 4 on the 12-clock you only ever visit {0, 4, 8}: a subgroup, a copy of ℤ/3ℤ inside ℤ/12ℤ.

1.1 Groups · What is ℤ/nℤ the symmetries of?

Decorate the polygon until only rotations survive

Using the clock, we might guess that ℤ/nℤ is the symmetries of a regular n-gon. Not quite: it is the rotations, a proper subgroup of Dn, because the reflections are missing.

The trick: draw a small arrow on every edge and insist that a symmetry keeps the arrows pointing the same way round. Reflections reverse the sense of travel, so they are no longer symmetries; the n rotations remain, and they compose exactly like ℤ/nℤ (turn j clicks, then k clicks, is j + k clicks, wrapped).

Looking ahead
This decorated polygon is the Cayley graph of ℤ/nℤ: one dot per element, one arrow per “add 1”. The same recipe builds an object for every group; Office Hour 2 proves the group is exactly its symmetry group.
{{fig:figarrowgon}}
Left: after a rotation the arrows still agree. Right: after a reflection every arrow is reversed, so the reflection is not a symmetry of the decorated polygon.
Animationoptional · rotate versus reflect the arrowed pentagon

A rotation carries every arrow to an arrow pointing the same way round; a reflection lands the polygon on itself but reverses every arrow.

1.2 Infinite groups · ℤ and ℤ²

The integers and the integer grid

Many first courses skip infinite groups. This is where the fun is, and where pictures earn their keep: you cannot write an infinite multiplication table, but you can draw the group's object.

ℤ
The integers under addition: identity 0, inverse of n is −n. Generated by {1}, and also by {2, 3} since 3 − 2 = 1. Mark the integer points on a line: the symmetries are translations by an integer and reflections about integer or half-integer points. ℤ is the translations. Draw arrows on the segments and the reflections disappear, so ℤ becomes the full symmetry group.
ℤ²
Pairs (m, n) of integers, added coordinate by coordinate, generated by (1, 0) and (0, 1). Colour the horizontal edges blue and the vertical edges green, add arrows, and demand that colours and arrows are preserved. A quarter turn would swap blue and green and a reflection would flip arrows, so only translations by lattice vectors survive: the symmetries are exactly ℤ².
Keep in mind
ℤ² is abelian. The free group two slides on also has two generators but is not. Two generators, two very different groups.

1.2 Infinite groups · Multiplicative and matrix groups

Numbers under multiplication, and GL(n, ℝ)

Multiplicative groups. ℝ× and ℂ× are the nonzero real and complex numbers under ordinary multiplication, identity 1. Zero is left out because it has no inverse.

Why they are not finitely generated
Finitely many numbers r₁, …, rk produce only the products r₁e₁⋯rkek with integer exponents: countably many. But ℝ× is uncountable. Every group met so far had a finite generating set; these do not.

Matrix groups. GL(n, ℝ), the general linear group, is the set of real n × n matrices with nonzero determinant. It is a group because: the product of two such matrices again has nonzero determinant, by det(MN) = det M · det N; the identity matrix has determinant 1; a matrix with nonzero determinant is invertible; and matrix multiplication is associative.

Relatives: SL(n, ℝ) (determinant 1, the special linear group), GL(n, ℤ) (integer entries, which forces determinant ±1), SL(n, ℤ), and the complex versions. Subgroups of GL(n, ℂ) are called linear groups.

Exercise 4
A matrix group over a finite field: GL(2, ℤ/2ℤ), the invertible 2 × 2 matrices with entries 0 and 1, has 6 elements and behaves like S₃.
Animationoptional · the determinant as an area factor

Apply M to the unit square, then N: the areas multiply, which is det(NM) = det N · det M made visible.

1.2 Infinite groups · The free group of rank 2 · Exercises 5 and 6

F₂: words with no rules except cancellation

Definitions
A word in the letters a, b is any finite string of the symbols a, b, a⁻¹, b⁻¹, the empty word included; two words are multiplied by concatenation. A word is reduced if no a stands next to an a⁻¹ and no b next to a b⁻¹. Deleting such a pair shortens a word, and repeating this reaches a reduced word.
F₂ is the set of reduced words, with multiplication “concatenate, then reduce”.
  • Identity: the empty word. Inverse: reverse the word and invert each letter, e.g. (aba⁻¹b)⁻¹ = b⁻¹ab⁻¹a⁻¹ (socks and shoes).
  • Associativity follows from one fact that needs proof: reducing a word always ends at the same reduced word, whatever order you cancel in.
  • F₂ is generated by {a, b}. It is not abelian: ab and ba are different reduced words, hence different elements.
Exercises 5 and 6
Uniqueness of the reduced word, by induction on length: if two different cancelling pairs are available and they do not overlap, cancel both in either order and the results agree; if they overlap, the word contains x x⁻¹ x and either cancellation leaves x. This is exactly what makes (uv)w = u(vw): both are reductions of the single string uvw.
Other free groups
The same construction on any alphabet S gives the free group F(S); its rank is the size of S. F₁ is just the powers of one letter, a copy of ℤ. Free groups matter because every group is a quotient of one (Section 1.4).

1.2 Infinite groups · The picture for F₂

A word is a walk that never backtracks

Read a word letter by letter starting at the centre: right for a, up for b, left for a⁻¹, down for b⁻¹. Cancelling xx⁻¹ is erasing a there-and-back. A reduced word is a walk that never backtracks.

The picture continues forever, every vertex has four neighbours, and there are no loops: an infinite tree. Distinct reduced words reach distinct vertices, because in a tree there is only one non-backtracking route to each vertex.

What this tree is
It is the Cayley graph of F₂, and Office Hour 2 shows that F₂ is exactly its group of symmetries preserving the letters and arrows. Compare: the arrowed polygon (ℤ/nℤ), the arrowed line (ℤ), the coloured grid (ℤ²). Same construction, different groups.
And many more
Coxeter groups, right-angled Artin groups, lamplighter groups, Thompson's groups, mapping class groups and braid groups all appear later in the book, each with its own picture.

1.3 Homomorphisms and normal subgroups · Homomorphisms

Maps that respect multiplication

Definition
A homomorphism from G to H is a function f : G → H with f(ab) = f(a)f(b) for all a, b. A bijective homomorphism is an isomorphism, and isomorphic groups are the same group with the elements renamed. Two consequences: f(1) = 1 and f(g⁻¹) = f(g)⁻¹.

Injective homomorphisms realise G as a subgroup of H: ℤ/nℤ → Dn (rotations), ℤ/2ℤ → Sn (a product of disjoint swaps), ℤ → ℤ² (a line in the lattice), ℤ → F₂ (the powers of a word). A homomorphism is injective exactly when only 1 maps to 1.

Non-injective homomorphisms deliberately forget information, keeping only what is consistent with multiplication:

mapremembers only
ℤ → ℤ/2ℤparity: even or odd
det : GL(n,ℝ) → ℝ×the determinant
ℝ× → ℤ/2ℤthe sign
Sn → ℤ/2ℤan even or odd number of crossings
Dn → ℤ/2ℤflipped over or not
F₂ → ℤ²the exponent sums of a and of b

1.3 · Normal subgroups and kernels

Conjugation, kernels, and why kernels are normal

Definitions
A normal subgroup of G is a subgroup N such that gng⁻¹ ∈ N for every n ∈ N and g ∈ G. The element gng⁻¹ is the conjugate of n by g: “n, seen from g's point of view” (move by g⁻¹, do n, move back).
The kernel of a homomorphism f is the set of elements f sends to the identity, as for the null space of a linear map.
Kernels are normal
If f(n) = 1 then f(gng⁻¹) = f(g) · 1 · f(g)⁻¹ = 1, so gng⁻¹ is in the kernel too.

Kernels of the forgetful maps: the even integers 2ℤ; SL(n, ℝ); the positive reals; the alternating group An (even permutations); the rotations of the polygon; the commutator subgroup [F₂, F₂], the words whose a-exponents and b-exponents both sum to zero.

Not every subgroup is normal
In D₃ the rotations are normal (a conjugate of a rotation is a rotation), but {1, s} is not: rsr⁻¹ is the reflection in the axis rotated by 120°, which lies outside {1, s}. In an abelian group gng⁻¹ = n, so there every subgroup is normal.

1.3 · Quotient groups · Exercise 9

Declare every element of N to be trivial

The quotient group G/N
Call g₁ ~ g₂ if g₁g₂⁻¹ ∈ N, and write [g] for the class of g. The classes are the cosets Ng; the class of 1 is N itself. G/N is the set of classes with multiplication [g₁][g₂] = [g₁g₂]. Informally: the group obtained from G by declaring every element of N to be trivial.
Exercise 9: the one computation to know
Change the representatives: (n₁g₁)(n₂g₂) = n₁ (g₁n₂g₁⁻¹) g₁g₂. This lies in the class of g₁g₂ exactly when g₁n₂g₁⁻¹ ∈ N. So the multiplication is well defined precisely when N is normal: the definition of “normal” is exactly what the quotient needs.

The map g ↦ [g] is a homomorphism G → G/N whose kernel is precisely N. So the loop closes: normal subgroups and surjective homomorphisms are the same thing. Whenever you meet one, the other is hiding behind it.

1.3 · The first isomorphism theorem · Exercise 10

Theorem 1.1: if f : G → H is onto with kernel K, then H ≅ G/K

Proof sketch
Define φ([g]) = f(g). Well defined: if [g] = [g′] then g′ = kg with k ∈ K, so f(g′) = f(k)f(g) = f(g). A homomorphism: φ([g][g′]) = f(gg′) = f(g)f(g′). Onto because f is. One-to-one: φ([g]) = 1 means g ∈ K, i.e. [g] = [1].

The examples, read as slogans:

  • ℤ/2ℤ = integers modulo the evens: parity. (This explains the notation.)
  • GL(n,ℝ)/SL(n,ℝ) ≅ ℝ×: matrices modulo “determinant 1” is the determinant.
  • Sn/An ≅ ℤ/2ℤ: even or odd. Dn/rotations ≅ ℤ/2ℤ: flipped or not.
  • F₂/[F₂, F₂] ≅ ℤ²: forcing ab = ba in the free group produces ℤ². This is the presentation story of the next section.
Exercise 10
Build your own: ℤ/6ℤ → ℤ/2ℤ by parity has kernel {0, 2, 4}, so (ℤ/6ℤ)/{0,2,4} ≅ ℤ/2ℤ. What do ℂ× → ℝ×, z ↦ |z|, and ℤ² → ℤ, (m, n) ↦ m − n, give?

1.4 Group presentations · Exercise 11

Generators plus a few defining equations

A multiplication table describes a group completely, but is hopeless for big or infinite groups. A generating set is more economical but not enough: F₂ and ℤ² both have two generators, and only one is abelian. The way out: some equations imply others (from gh = k and h = pq follows gpq = k), so a short list of defining equations can pin down the whole table.

Examples
ℤ/nℤ ≅ ⟨a | aⁿ = 1⟩: every table entry follows from the one rule, e.g. aⁿ⁻³a⁴ = aⁿ⁺¹ = aⁿ·a = a.   ℤ² ≅ ⟨x, y | xy = yx⟩: two generators and the single rule that they commute (Exercise 11: every word sorts to xmyn).
The formal definition
A presentation is a pair (S, R): an alphabet S and a set R of words in it. The group ⟨S | R⟩ is the free group F(S) divided by the normal closure of R, the smallest normal subgroup containing R. Elements of S are generators; elements of R are defining relators. A relation u = v becomes the relator uv⁻¹: ab = ba becomes aba⁻¹b⁻¹.
Why the normal closure?
If a word r is to equal 1, then so must grg⁻¹ for every g, and products and inverses of such elements as well. The set of all consequences is therefore a normal subgroup containing R, and the smallest one is the normal closure. Dividing by a non-normal subgroup would not even give a group.

In the quotient you may insert the relator anywhere. Insert it in front of ba, regroup, and the middle cancels: ba has become ab. Repeating sorts any word into ambn, which is the element (m, n) of ℤ².

1.4 · What a presentation looks like · Exercises 12 and 13

Relators are the loops in the Cayley graph

One dot per element; from each dot an arrow per generator s, from g to gs (drawn two-way when s = s⁻¹). Reading the labels along a path spells a word, and a closed loop spells a word equal to 1: a relator.

Every group is a quotient of a free group
Take the whole group as alphabet, S = G. The map F(G) → G sending each letter to the element it names is a homomorphism and is onto. By Theorem 1.1, G ≅ F(G)/K with K the kernel, so G ≅ ⟨G | K⟩. Wasteful, but a presentation: this is Exercise 12, and the reason free groups appear everywhere in the book.
Exercise 13: presentations to find
Fn = ⟨a₁, …, an | ⟩ with no relators. Dn = ⟨s, t | s², t², (st)ⁿ⟩ from the two reflections, or ⟨r, s | rⁿ, s², srs⁻¹r⟩ from a rotation and a reflection. Sn: generators the neighbour swaps σi = (i i+1), relators σi², (σiσi+1)³, and (σiσj)² for |i − j| ≥ 2.
Presentations have limits
There is no algorithm that decides whether two finite presentations describe isomorphic groups (Adian, Rabin). Even deciding whether a given word equals 1 is impossible in general (Novikov, Boone). Geometry rescues large classes of groups: Office Hour 9.
